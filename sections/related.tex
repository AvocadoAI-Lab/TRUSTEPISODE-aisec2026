\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Provenance-Based Detection and Evidence Fabric}

Provenance-based intrusion detection systems (PIDS) construct causal graphs in which nodes represent system entities and edges capture information-flow or execution relations. Ning et al.~\cite{ning2002} first formalized the correlation of intrusion alerts into coherent attack scenarios through logic-based prerequisite/consequence rules; this is the direct academic origin of \trustepisode's episode-linking function $L(e_i,e_j)$ (Section~\ref{sec:episode-synthesis}). Graph representation learning approaches improve the fidelity of provenance graph modeling, such as masked graph autoencoders~\cite{magic2024} and online fine-grained subgraph detectors~\cite{nodlink2024}. This line extends further toward runtime, whole-system provenance analysis, using techniques ranging from graph sketching with statistical clustering~\cite{unicorn2020} to graph neural encoder--decoder architectures with learned anomaly scores~\cite{kairos2024}. CAPTAIN adapts rule-based PIDS by optimizing node initialization, edge propagation, and alarm thresholds while retaining low overhead and interpretability~\cite{captain2025}. A complementary direction targets identity-centric environments: HADES performs whole-network provenance analytics to trace identity-triggered, cross-machine causal chains in Active Directory settings~\cite{hades2024}. However, many provenance detectors---including recent whole-system systems such as Kairos, and identity-focused systems such as HADES---remain centered on a single evidence source and issue alarms at the node, edge, or subgraph level. They do not directly produce calibrated, cross-source, episode-level operational risk scores such as \trustepisode's $R_\epsilon = P_\epsilon \times I_\epsilon$.

\subsection{Adaptive Correlation and Risk-Based Security Operations}

Alert fatigue is a well-documented and severe operational problem: analysts routinely face alert volumes that exceed triage capacity~\cite{alertfatigue2025}. Recent systems address this at scale: GraphWeaver correlates billions of security events in production environments~\cite{graphweaver2024}, AlertPro applies reinforcement learning for context-aware alert prioritization under multi-step attack scenarios~\cite{alertpro2024}, and BiCAM correlates heterogeneous security events across different devices and detection platforms via bidirectional attention~\cite{bicam2025}. Yet alert counts and vendor severity labels are not synonymous with operational risk. A high-confidence indicator match can be irrelevant to the affected asset; conversely, several low-severity events can become significant when they form a coherent sequence. Alert-correlation and multi-information-fusion approaches demonstrate this pattern directly, showing that corroboration across independent sources can elevate confidence in otherwise low-severity signals~\cite{alertcorr2025,multistep2024}. Neither, however, converts this corroboration into a calibrated probability or an episode-level, explainable score decomposition.

\trustepisode therefore models risk at the episode level and separates maliciousness likelihood $P_\epsilon$ from operational impact $I_\epsilon$ (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}). The model learns locally appropriate contributions while preserving explicit semantic factors and policy constraints. CTI can provide auxiliary context for episode correlation, such as technique mappings, indicator context, and source confidence; rather than a flat tag match, CTI knowledge can be structured and aligned with provenance evidence~\cite{knowhow2026}. However, we do not treat CTI itself as the primary detection or scoring contribution; instead, it is used only as bounded corroborative evidence that feeds into the $C_\epsilon$ and $T_\epsilon$ factors alongside other cross-source evidence.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Analysis and Security Agents}
\label{sec:rag-agents}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves the grounding of language-model outputs by augmenting generation with retrieved evidence~\cite{rag2020}. In a security setting, however, direct LLM scoring does not by itself provide a calibrated risk estimate; moreover, citation alone does not guarantee faithful grounding, since a generated narrative can cite a source while still misrepresenting its content~\cite{correctness2024}. \trustepisode uses Agentic RAG only for assurance: evidence retrieval, contradiction discovery, gap identification, and citation-aware reporting. The agent is read-only with respect to the primary score and enforcement actions, an execution-isolation boundary consistent with the architectural principle proposed by IsolateGPT~\cite{isolategpt2025}.

\subsection{Calibration, Robustness, and Explanation}

Risk ranking and risk calibration solve different problems. A model can rank malicious episodes near the top while systematically overestimating or underestimating their probability. This distinction matters when a score determines escalation thresholds, analyst attention, and the amount of evidence demanded before a potentially disruptive response. Guo et al.~\cite{guo2017calib} showed that high classification accuracy does not imply well-calibrated probabilities. Focal loss can optimize calibration directly during training~\cite{mukhoti2020focal}, and cybersecurity-specific work extends calibration analysis to threat scores~\cite{classifiercalib2023}. Reliability diagrams and proper score decompositions~\cite{reliability2021} provide the evaluation protocol \trustepisode adopts for its calibration function $\mathrm{Calibrate}(\sigma(z_\epsilon))$ (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}). \trustepisode therefore uses calibration loss and reliability analysis as first-class evaluation targets rather than treating an anomaly value as a probability by default.

Robustness is similarly distinct from raw classification performance. Explicit uncertainty quantification can improve trustworthiness in ML-based network intrusion detection when evidence is incomplete or out-of-distribution; however, existing work addresses uncertainty at the level of a single classifier rather than the episode-level propagation problem of how a score should jointly lose confidence when multiple heterogeneous sources are missing~\cite{uncertainty2023}. In real operations, a network sensor may be absent, identity events may arrive late, asset inventory may be stale, and CTI may contain weak or contradictory indicators. We design the model so that absence of evidence reduces confidence through an explicit penalty $M_\epsilon$ (Section~\ref{sec:features}) rather than silently increasing the likelihood of a threat. The final score can therefore be accompanied by a caveat such as ``high priority, but reduced confidence because NDR corroboration is unavailable.'' This is a more actionable output than a single opaque anomaly score.

\paragraph{Explainable Risk Decomposition.} A risk score must not only be calibrated but also decomposable and auditable. Security risk assessment commonly considers likelihood and impact as central inputs to risk determination, as reflected in NIST SP~800-30~\cite{nist80030}. \trustepisode operationalizes this principle as $R_\epsilon = P_\epsilon \times I_\epsilon$. On the explainability side, graph representations combined with Shapley-value attribution have been used to provide factor-level explanation, prioritization, and triage of security alerts, letting analysts see not just a ranking but each factor's concrete contribution to the final judgment~\cite{cybershapley2025}. Finally, the statistical grounding of the risk score itself also has established precedent. Yen et al. demonstrated a logistic-regression model that infers enterprise host risk of malware encounter from heterogeneous factors spanning user demographics, VPN activity, and web behavior; hosts ranked in the top tier by the model encountered malware at a rate substantially higher than the enterprise-wide base rate~\cite{yen2014epidemiological}. Later work combines host features from both file-based and network-based monitoring perspectives, training several classifiers including logistic regression to predict whether a host will execute malware in the coming week, and argues that simple, interpretable models such as logistic regression are better suited to operational use than black-box alternatives when analyst judgment is required~\cite{chi2023remediation}. \trustepisode adopts the standard likelihood-times-impact decomposition, follows this methodological line of combining multiple heterogeneous factors into a single risk score with statistical discipline while deliberately favoring interpretable models. The final episode probability is calibrated, while factor functions follow the monotonicity and semantic constraints defined in the scoring model; CTI influence is explicitly bounded.

In summary, our positioning relative to prior work rests on a few clear lines. We do not claim novelty in the observation that provenance graphs support causal attack reconstruction~\cite{ning2002,captain2025,kairos2024}, that CTI can enrich detection when structured appropriately~\cite{knowhow2026}, that likelihood and impact are central inputs to risk determination~\cite{nist80030}, that graph representations with Shapley-value attribution can provide factor-level alert explanation~\cite{cybershapley2025}, that retrieval-augmented generation improves the grounding of language-model outputs~\cite{rag2020}, or that alert correlation reduces analyst load at scale~\cite{graphweaver2024,alertpro2024,alertfatigue2025}. These are established findings we build upon rather than originate. Our novelty lies specifically in: (i) fusing five heterogeneous evidence sources---endpoint, network, identity, asset context, and CTI---into a single episode-level evidence graph rather than a host-centric or single-domain one; (ii) treating probability calibration and explicit missing-evidence robustness as first-class design objectives (Sections~\ref{sec:features}--\ref{sec:scoring}) rather than post-hoc adjustments to a ranking score; and (iii) constraining Agentic RAG to evidence assurance---retrieval, contradiction discovery, and citation-aware narration---while keeping it strictly read-only with respect to the numerical score and enforcement actions (Section~\ref{sec:agentic-assurance}). This governance boundary is discussed in full in Section~\ref{sec:rag-agents} and is only restated here for positioning purposes.
